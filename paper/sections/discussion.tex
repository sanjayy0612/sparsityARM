\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.} On equal-work block-aligned masks,
packed B8 execution overtakes Accelerate dense only at 40--50\% sparsity,
well beyond what the tested models tolerate. The same masks executed
neuron-by-neuron with neuron-major weights are faster than dense at the
quality ceiling, by a few percent. Block alignment is therefore useful on this
CPU, but its value comes from keeping active weights in long contiguous runs,
not from packing blocks into short tiles. The quality ceiling, not kernel
efficiency, limits the achievable gain: at 10--20\% sparsity even a perfect
kernel could save only 11--25\% of FFN time.

\paragraph{Where the bottleneck is.} The structure tax in
Section~\ref{sec:quality}---a 30\% budget costs 0.2--0.3\% perplexity at
neuron granularity but 6.9--20.2\% at B8---shows that the missing ingredient is
a \emph{representation} in which useful neurons are contiguous. Post-hoc
reordering moved the quality frontier only slightly. More plausible routes are
training-time or fine-tuning methods that make activations block-sparse by
construction, such as natively trained mixture-of-experts (MoE) FFNs,
ReLU-restoring fine-tuning~\cite{relustrikes}, or joint selector and layout
learning. Any such continuation should be compared against quantized dense
baselines, which are already roughly three times faster than FP32 here.

\paragraph{On reporting negative results.} Fixing the quality gate and grids
in version control before running each experiment~\cite{prereg} prevented
the gate from being loosened once results were known, and retaining every
completed experiment, including failed variants, in the artifact lets readers
verify that the headline result is not the product of selective reporting.
The per-neuron finding itself was already present in the recorded data; it
became visible only when every executor in the artifact was compared against
the quality frontier, which is an argument for releasing all raw results.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Small quality screen.} 16 excerpts and 2,032 tokens, perplexity
  only, no downstream tasks. Exact ceilings may shift with a larger corpus.
\item \textbf{Oracle masks.} Selection reads dense activations that a
  deployable predictor cannot see, so real predictors are expected to do
  worse. The score ranks blocks independently, however, so a better subset
  could exist; we do not claim the oracle is optimal.
\item \textbf{Synthetic runtime masks and weights.} Break-even sweeps use random
  block-aligned masks and random weights. At Llama B8/10\% they agreed with
  real masks for packed B8; real masks gave a smaller per-neuron gain
  (0.9--3.5\% versus 2.6--7.3\%). This was not checked at other sparsities or
  for TinyLlama.
\item \textbf{Uncontrolled system state.} macOS does not allow pinning a thread
  to a performance core, and we did not control frequency, thermal state, or
  power source. Dense medians varied by up to 17\% across EXP-012 cases.
  Randomized executor order limits bias between executors but not
  run-to-run variation.
\item \textbf{Narrow hardware scope.} One Apple M2, one thread, one token, FP32
  kernels, two FFN geometries. Multi-threaded, batched, or GPU/NPU execution
  may behave differently.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Reported ranges are across three repeated runs, not
  confidence intervals. The smallest positive result (0.9\% on real masks) is
  within plausible run-to-run noise even though it was positive in every run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The artifact contains source code, pinned model revisions, raw per-sample
timings, per-record log-likelihoods, manifests with hashes, independent
validators, and the scripts that regenerate the main figures and tables in
this paper. The committed result package can be validated without downloading
either model.
