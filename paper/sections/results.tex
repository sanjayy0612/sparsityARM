\subsection{Quality frontier}
\label{sec:quality}

Figure~\ref{fig:quality} and Table~\ref{tab:grid} show the quality frontier.
For Llama 3.2 1B, B8/10\% increased perplexity by 2.52\% and passed; 20\%
(+8.77\%) and 30\% (+20.17\%) failed. TinyLlama was more tolerant, passing at
10\% (+1.34\%) and 20\% (+3.16\%) before failing at 30\% (+6.87\%). Coarser
blocks were worse: Llama B16 missed the gate at 10\% (+5.16\%), and TinyLlama
B32 passed only at 10\% (+3.62\%), failing at 20\% (+10.31\%). Because the
degradation grows monotonically, we did not measure quality at 40\% and
beyond.

\paragraph{The structure tax.} The quality loss is attributable mainly to
contiguity rather than to sparsity itself. At the same 30\% budget, neuron-level
top-$k$ oracle masks changed perplexity by only +0.21\% (Llama, EXP-003) and
+0.29\% (TinyLlama, EXP-016), whereas B8 blocks cost +20.17\% and +6.87\%.
Both models contain ample \emph{unstructured} contextual sparsity; forcing it
into groups of eight adjacent neurons destroys most of it. In the EXP-003
screen, random B8 masks at 30\% raised Llama perplexity by 1,536\% versus
+21.3\% for activation-ranked blocks, so block scoring carries real
information---it is simply not enough.

\subsection{Runtime frontier}
\label{sec:runtime}

\paragraph{Packed B8.} For the Llama geometry, packed B8 was 8.9--11.7\%
slower than dense at 30\% sparsity and first became faster at the 40\% grid
point, by 1.6--3.1\% (Figure~\ref{fig:speed}). For the TinyLlama geometry it
was still 1.1--5.2\% slower at 40\% and first won at 50\%, by 10.7--17.1\%.
The exact crossovers lie between grid points and were not interpolated. We did
not isolate why the smaller FFN needs more sparsity to break even.

\paragraph{Per-neuron on the same masks.} The per-neuron executor was faster
than dense at every measured block-aligned sparsity, in every run. At the
quality ceilings it gained 2.6--7.3\% (Llama, 10\%) and 5.1--7.3\% (TinyLlama,
20\%); at 30\% it gained about 25\% and 18\% (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). Since
both sparse executors do identical arithmetic, the roughly 30-point difference
between them at low sparsity comes from memory layout and loop structure
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

\paragraph{Real masks.} At the quality-compatible Llama condition (B8/10\%),
we replayed real recorded masks (7,376 of 8,192 neurons active; 922 active
blocks in a median of 93 contiguous runs). Dense took 4.01--4.13\,ms per FFN.
The per-neuron executor took 3.93--4.10\,ms, a speedup of 0.9--3.5\% in every
run. Packed B8 took 5.54--5.74\,ms, 37--39\% longer than dense (a speedup of
$-27.1\%$ to $-27.9\%$), agreeing closely with the synthetic masks at the same
sparsity ($-26.8\%$ to $-28.7\%$).

\paragraph{Alignment matters for the per-neuron executor too.} In an earlier
synthetic sweep (EXP-001) with the same per-neuron kernel, masks that selected
individual neurons uniformly at random were slower than dense in 10 of 12
configurations at 20--40\% sparsity (e.g.\ 5.28 versus 4.36\,ms median at
30\%). The per-neuron speedups above therefore depend on active neurons
arriving in aligned runs, which is exactly what block masks provide.

For the packed B8 executor the intersection is empty
(Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}, Table~\ref{tab:key}): its first measured speedup
lies three grid points (30 percentage points) beyond the highest passing
point for both models. Because the grid spacing is 10 points, the true gap is
at least 10 points. For the per-neuron executor the intersection is
\emph{non-empty but small}: B8/10\% for Llama and B8/20\% for TinyLlama pass
the quality gate and are faster than dense in every run, by 0.9--3.5\% on real
Llama masks and 2.6--7.3\% and 5.1--7.3\% on synthetic masks.
